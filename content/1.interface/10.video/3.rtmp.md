SportyPlus does not need a particular make of camera. Anything that can **push an
RTMP stream** to an address you give it will work — most IP cameras, encoders and
NVRs can, and so can software like OBS.

What differs by make is only where the settings live and what they are called.
The values below are the same for all of them.

::note
**What you give up without DoLynk.** A Dahua camera on DoLynk Cloud also keeps
its own copy on an SD card, so a match interrupted by a connection drop can be
completed afterwards from the camera. Any other camera has no second copy: if the
club's internet drops mid-match, that part of the recording is gone for good.

You also lose remote configuration. Everything on this page has to be set in the
camera's own interface, on the venue network — SportyPlus cannot read or change
any of it. See [Dahua cameras](/video/dahua) for what the alternative looks like.
::

## The settings

| Setting | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Video codec | **H.264** — never H.265/HEVC |
| Keyframe interval | **1 second** |
| Resolution | **1920×1080** |
| Frame rate | **30** (25 in PAL installs), fixed |
| Rate control | **CBR** |
| Bitrate | **4096 kbps** (never above 6144) |
| Audio codec | **AAC**, or no audio at all |
| Profile | **High** or **Baseline** |

Everything below explains why, and what goes wrong when one is missed.

## Video codec — H.264 only

::warning
**H.265 (HEVC) produces no video at all, and says nothing about it.** The camera
connects, the stream is accepted, and no picture ever arrives — not in live view,
and not in the recording. There is no error message anywhere to point at it.

If a camera streams nothing at all and everything else looks right, this is the
first thing to check.
::

Set the codec to **H.264**. If the camera offers a profile, **High** is right;
**Baseline** is also fine and displays a fraction sooner.

## Keyframe interval — one second

This is the setting that decides how far behind live view runs, and the one most
likely to be wrong out of the box.

A viewer can only join the video at a *keyframe*, and live video is cut into
chunks on keyframe boundaries. A camera sending a keyframe once every 25 seconds
produces 25-second chunks, and live view ends up **more than a minute behind** the
court. One keyframe per second brings that to about three seconds.

Cameras express this in one of two ways, and it is worth checking which:

- **In frames** — called *I Frame Interval*, *GOP*, *GOP length* or *keyframe
  interval*. Set it to **the same number as the frame rate**: 30 at 30fps, 25 at
  25fps.
- **In seconds** — set it to **1**.

Going shorter buys nothing: chunks cannot get smaller than a second no matter
what the camera does, and the extra keyframes come out of picture quality. Going
longer costs you directly — a 2-second interval means about six seconds behind,
and it grows from there.

::note
Some cameras have a "smart" or "adaptive" mode — Dahua calls it Smart Codec or
H.264+, other makes call it Smart Encoding, H.265+ or similar — that holds back
keyframes when the picture is not moving. On a court between points that is most
of the time. **Turn it off.** It overrides the interval above, and an idle court
is its worst case.
::

## Resolution, frame rate and bitrate

**1920×1080 at 30fps, 4096 kbps, CBR.**

Nothing is re-encoded between the camera and a viewer, so every person watching
pulls the full bitrate the camera sends. Doubling it doubles what the club's
connection has to carry for each of them, and a stream the uplink cannot sustain
drops out — which costs the match, not just the sharpness. **Do not exceed 6144
kbps** at this resolution without asking us first.

Use **CBR** rather than VBR. Nothing smooths a variable bitrate's spikes before
they reach a viewer, and those spikes land exactly when the match is worth
watching.

Use a **fixed** frame rate. Variable frame rate makes chunk lengths uneven, and
uneven chunks slow live view for everyone watching that court.

Avoid **4K** and **50 or 60fps**. Neither breaks anything by itself, but both
multiply what every viewer downloads, and the vendor presets that offer them
often carry long keyframe intervals.

## Audio

::warning
**Only AAC works.** Cameras frequently default to **G.711**, **G.726** or
similar, and those are accepted all the way along — the camera connects, the
video plays, and the sound is simply never there. Nothing reports an error, so it
reads as a broken microphone rather than a setting.
::

A camera with no microphone, or with audio switched off, is fine. It streams and
records normally without an audio track.

## Pointing the camera at SportyPlus

In **Club Admin → Commercial → Cameras**, create the camera:

- **Camera type** — *Pushes RTMP to us*
- **Court** — the court the camera actually points at

::warning
Double-check the court. A camera mapped to the wrong court records the wrong
match, and nobody notices until someone watches one back — by which point the
footage they wanted is gone.
::

Save, reopen it, and copy the **stream address**. It looks like:

```
rtmp://stream1.sporty.plus/stream/<playback-id>?key=<stream-key>
```

Put that in the camera's RTMP settings — usually under *Network*, *Streaming* or
*Publish* — and enable it. Some cameras split the address into a **server URL**
and a **stream key**; where they do:

- **Server / URL** — `rtmp://stream1.sporty.plus/stream`
- **Stream key** — `<playback-id>?key=<stream-key>`, exactly as given, including
  the `?key=` part

::warning
**The `?key=` part is not optional.** Without it, or with one character wrong,
the stream is refused and most cameras retry silently forever with no useful
error of their own — so a mistyped address looks like a network problem.

Every camera has its own address and key. They cannot be shared or reused between
cameras: two cameras on one key cannot both stream.
::

If the camera pushes more than one stream, point it at the one you configured
above — usually called the **main** or **primary** stream. A camera publishing its
secondary stream sends none of these settings, which looks exactly like the
settings not having taken.

## Checking it works

1. **Live view** — open the camera in Club Admin. The picture should appear within
   a few seconds and stay **about three seconds** behind the court. A minute or
   more behind means the keyframe interval is too long, or smart encoding is on.
2. **A real recording** — play back a reservation recorded on that court.

## If something is wrong

| What you see | Usually means |
| --- | --- |
| Live view is a minute or more behind | Keyframe interval too long, or smart encoding on |
| Live view never appears, stream looks connected | The codec is H.265, not H.264 |
| Live view never appears at all | Stream address wrong, or the camera cannot reach the internet |
| Live view stutters or drops out | Bitrate too high for the club's upload |
| Video plays but there is no sound | Audio codec is not AAC |
| Settings appear to do nothing | The camera is publishing its secondary stream |
| A match has a hole in it | The connection dropped — without an SD card there is nothing to recover |

::note
**Why delay can drift upward on its own.** Live view runs as far behind as the
*longest* chunk in the last few seconds, not the average. A camera that mostly
sends keyframes on time but occasionally misses one sits at the slower figure
until it stops missing them.

So live view that "randomly got worse" is usually the camera's keyframes rather
than the club's internet — check the keyframe interval before anything else.
::
